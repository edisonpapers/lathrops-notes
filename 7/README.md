# Section 4 - Beyond the House

## Staff Organization and Work Beyond Glenmont

`image src=https://npgallery.nps.gov/EDIS/GetAsset/dfd8d77c9e604b2a90152f432ff65444/proxyhires.jpg .right alt="A hayride for the Edison children in front of Glenmont, with a horse-drawn wagon on the estate grounds." caption="A hayride for the children in front of Glenmont, 1897. This image shows the estate as a place of family recreation, but outdoor leisure depended on the labor of workers who maintained horses, vehicles, roads, lawns, gardens, and service buildings. Thomas Edison NHP Historical Photograph Collection, Thomas Edison National Historical Park, West Orange, New Jersey. Image credit: U.S. National Park Service. Public domain."`

[Source: NPGallery, “A hayride for the children in front of Glenmont.”](https://npgallery.nps.gov/EDIS/AssetDetail/dfd8d77c-9e60-4b2a-9015-2f432ff65444)

Male employees usually worked outside the main domestic interior and were generally listed separately on payroll records under categories such as “greenhouse” and “stable.” These workers included gardeners, chauffeurs, watchmen, coachmen, and greenhouse employees. Their labor helped maintain the parts of Glenmont that extended beyond the rooms of the house: lawns, roads, gardens, stables, garages, greenhouses, outbuildings, and vehicles. Contemporary horticultural publications, such as this [period gardening volume in the Biodiversity Heritage Library](https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/item/132781#page/6/mode/1up), help contextualize the specialized knowledge associated with greenhouse and landscape work.

The gardener was often among the highest-paid estate employees. Some male staff lived on or near the property with their families, including gardener Ernest Walters, who resided near the greenhouse, and chauffeurs who likely occupied quarters associated with the garage. These arrangements show that estate labor extended beyond the walls of Glenmont and, for some workers, blurred the line between workplace and home.

### Grounds Maintenance: Michael Doyle and George Atkinson

Michael C. Doyle maintained the estate grounds during the early twentieth century. Identified in correspondence between 1908 and 1911 and later described in a 1914 obituary as Thomas Edison’s gardener of roughly ten years, Doyle’s responsibilities extended across grounds, fuel, and environmental management. At Seminole Lodge in Fort Myers, Florida, he stacked firewood in the grate, tended to practical needs of heating and outdoor maintenance, and moved between interior and exterior thresholds.

Doyle’s work reveals how “men’s labor” on the estate was not limited to lawns and gardens. Gardens, piers, grates, fuel, service areas, and outdoor circulation all required physical endurance and practical oversight. If the houseman safeguarded stored materials inside the house, the gardener managed the living environment beyond the walls.

George Atkinson represents a later phase of Glenmont’s grounds work. After Ernest Walters left Glenmont in 1921, Atkinson became head gardener and remained in that role until his death in 1936. He appears to have worked closely with Mina Edison, who took an active interest in gardening. In the 1930s, Mina and Atkinson collaborated on horticultural experiments in the rose garden, including the development of a rose variety that Mina named “Valinda” in honor of her mother.

Together, Doyle and Atkinson show how gardening at Glenmont was both practical and expressive. Grounds workers maintained grass, flowers, greenhouse plants, trees, roads, and outdoor work areas, but they also helped produce the estate’s atmosphere of beauty, order, and refinement.

### Chauffeurs, Automobiles, and the Garage

`image src=https://npgallery.nps.gov/EDIS/GetAsset/a1eed6aa5cbf4ebba3ecf2a3f3cd0412/proxyhires.jpg .right alt="A man seated in an automobile at the entrance to the Glenmont garage." caption="Glenmont garage, man seated in automobile at entrance, date unknown. [Source: NPGallery, “Glenmont, garage, man seated in automobile at entrance.”](https://npgallery.nps.gov/EDIS/AssetDetail/a1eed6aa-5cbf-4ebb-a3ec-f2a3f3cd0412)

The chauffeur’s work connected Glenmont to the world beyond Llewellyn Park. As automobiles became part of elite household life, transportation labor shifted from the stable and coachman to the garage and chauffeur. Chauffeurs did more than drive. They maintained cars, prepared vehicles for trips, managed timing, handled mechanical problems, and helped coordinate family movement between Glenmont, the West Orange laboratory, train stations, social visits, and seasonal travel.

At Glenmont, chauffeur work also had a residential dimension. Some automobile-related employees likely lived in or near the garage, making the garage not only a storage and repair space but also part of the estate’s labor geography. Records from the twentieth century identify several chauffeurs connected to the Edison household or surrounding estate network, including Frank Mason, George J. Williams, and later Sidney Scarth.

Sidney Scarth offers one example of the chauffeur as a skilled household employee. Born in England, Scarth came to the United States as a child and worked as an auto mechanic before entering Edison service. By 1940, he was listed as Mina Edison’s chauffeur, working a full year and earning more than the butler and maid recorded in the same census. His position shows the growing importance of automobile labor within an elite household. The car was not simply a convenience; it required technical knowledge, regular maintenance, and a trusted worker who could manage both machines and family mobility.

# Section 5 - Rest and Respite

The third floor at Glenmont was where many live-in domestic workers slept, stored personal belongings, and found moments of privacy away from the formal rooms below. These rooms suggest that staff accommodations were clean, orderly, and properly furnished. They included beds, chairs, rugs, dressers, washstands, radiators, windows, and small personal or decorative objects.

Yet rest at Glenmont was never fully separate from work. Domestic workers lived inside the same house they served, and the architecture of the third floor kept them close to the household’s needs. The annunciator system and signal bells made this especially clear. Even in their rooms, staff could be summoned by bells from other parts of the house. Rest was possible, but it remained conditional.

## Living Where You Work

`image src=https://npgallery.nps.gov/EDIS/GetAsset/b42e13e35b13495394a113671e8e0b31/proxyhires.jpg .right alt="Interior view of Glenmont’s third-floor hall." caption="Glenmont, interior, third floor, hall, October 21, 1960. The third-floor hall connected the servant bedrooms and work spaces, making it both a circulation route and a reminder that staff life remained close to the working structure of the house. Thomas Edison NHP Historical Photograph Collection, Thomas Edison National Historical Park, West Orange, New Jersey. Image credit: U.S. National Park Service. Public domain."`

[Source: NPGallery, “Glenmont, interior, third floor, hall.”](https://npgallery.nps.gov/EDIS/AssetDetail/b42e13e3-5b13-4953-94a1-13671e8e0b31)

The third floor offered domestic workers a degree of separation from the public rooms of Glenmont. Unlike the kitchen, laundry, pantry, or dining room, these spaces were not primarily designed for production or display. They offered a place to sleep, sit, dress, keep personal belongings, and withdraw from the more visible work of the house.

But the third floor was not outside the labor system. Glenmont’s annunciator system connected family rooms, service spaces, and staff movement through bells and signals. In Mr. Charles’s third-floor room, a call button connected to the main annunciator board in the kitchen, where the station was labeled “Third South.” A signal bell in that room was also connected to a button in Thomas and Mina Edison’s second-floor bedroom. This meant that even upstairs rooms could be tied into the household’s system of summons, response, and service.

`image src=https://npgallery.nps.gov/EDIS/GetAsset/492c85f61f9141ab87765799879f775c/proxyhires.jpg .left alt="Interior view of a third-floor bedroom at Glenmont." caption="Glenmont, interior, third floor, bedroom, October 21, 1960. The room is modest but furnished, showing the kind of clean and orderly accommodation available in the upper-floor servant spaces. Thomas Edison NHP Historical Photograph Collection, Thomas Edison National Historical Park, West Orange, New Jersey. Image credit: U.S. National Park Service. Public domain."`

[Source: NPGallery, “Glenmont, interior, third floor, bedroom.”](https://npgallery.nps.gov/EDIS/AssetDetail/492c85f6-1f91-41ab-8776-5799879f775c)

These rooms complicate any simple story about servant life at Glenmont. They were not neglected spaces. The furnishings suggest that live-in workers had access to clean, heated, and usable accommodations. At the same time, the location of these rooms inside the working house meant that privacy was limited. A servant could rest, but not fully leave work behind. The bell could always ring.

The third floor was therefore both refuge and workplace. It gave domestic workers a place of their own, but that place remained within the sound, movement, and expectations of the Edison household.

### Bathrooms, Cleanliness, and the Working Environment

`image src=https://npgallery.nps.gov/EDIS/GetAsset/bf0955feba514eccbe1ad1281a7da76b/proxyhires.jpg .right alt="Interior view of Glenmont’s second-floor bathroom off the hall." caption="Glenmont, interior, second floor, bathroom off hall, October 21, 1960. [Source: NPGallery, “Glenmont, interior, second floor, bathroom off hall.”](https://npgallery.nps.gov/EDIS/AssetDetail/bf0955fe-ba51-4ecc-be1a-d1281a7da76b)

Bathrooms at Glenmont were signs of comfort, hygiene, and modern domestic life, but they were also working spaces. Sinks, tubs, mirrors, floors, towels, and fixtures required repeated attention. For staff living and working in the house, access to bathrooms was part of the material environment of domestic service. These rooms suggest a household that provided clean, functional accommodations, even as employees remained within the daily demands of service.

Cleanliness was also a learned practice. Domestic service required knowledge of how to sweep, scrub, dust, wash, polish, and keep rooms sanitary. A comparative photograph from The Henry Ford, [“Industrial School, Girls Sweeping and Reading a Lesson, 1890–1915”](https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/artifact/385313?AssetId=THF38042), shows how domestic skills could be taught as formal training. While this image is not connected to Glenmont, it helps contextualize the kinds of practical knowledge expected of women and girls entering household labor.

#### Transcription excerpt

“Anna and Theodore dined with us tonight and we had no cook. I have been trying and trying to get some one but it is impossible to get them to leave New York now. I am trying Anna one on Monday but I am not sure. Of course that she will be alright. My two nice upstairs girls have gone to Columbia to learn English and social service and then return in the spring to Russia to help their people. They were exceptional girls and I miss them.

Lena is my old standby and Henry - Bessie has been a trump so far in helping out the situation. Mrs. Weisman, who has had charge of our Florida houses, is being married this winter too so that will change conditions in Florida. I shall miss her old tennumbany. She has taken over the whole responsibility again there since Mr. Weisman’s death. You see I am having many complications. I am sorry that you got so tired working but Margaret and Ida assure me that you are better tonight. Bonnie and I had a nice visit together over Labor Day. I wish that I could see more of you all but we are so far apart. Thomas will not go and I can’t leave him alone. It is like sewing torn towels filled up and when your heart’s call is upon your time it is hopeless.”

This letter gives visitors a clearer sense of Glenmont as a working environment. Mina’s household depended on a combination of long-term trusted employees and shorter-term workers whose lives, ambitions, and obligations extended beyond the estate. Her “two nice upstairs girls” had left to study English and social service at Columbia before returning to Russia, suggesting that domestic service could intersect with education, mobility, and plans for future community work. At the same time, their absence created a practical problem for Mina, who was struggling to find a cook and keep the household running.

The letter also shows the importance of long-term staff. Lena, Henry, and Bessie appear as people Mina relied on when the household was short-staffed. Glenmont may have offered clean accommodations, structured work, and opportunities for some employees to pursue self-improvement, but it was also a demanding workplace. Maintaining comfort for the Edison family required constant adjustment: hiring from New York, managing absences, depending on experienced workers, and asking remaining staff to help “out the situation.

## Lucy Bogue: Companion, Teacher, and Trusted Household Presence

`image src=https://npgallery.nps.gov/EDIS/GetAsset/48f5a261bf034cab9360ac5d61152543/proxymdres.jpg .right alt="Theodore Edison as a boy playing the piano in the Den at Glenmont, with Miss Lucy Bogue seated beside him." caption="Theodore Edison as a boy playing the piano in the Den at Glenmont, with Miss Lucy Bogue at his side, 1907. [Source: NPGallery, “Theodore Edison as a boy playing the piano in Den at Glenmont, Miss Lucy Bogue at his side.”](https://npgallery.nps.gov/EDIS/AssetDetail/48f5a261-bf03-4cab-9360-ac5d61152543)

Lucy Bogue occupied an unusual position in the Edison household. She first entered the family’s orbit as a music teacher for the Edison children, but over time became a trusted companion, family associate, and source of emotional support for Mina Edison. She was not a domestic worker in the same sense as a cook, chambermaid, laundress, or parlor maid. Yet she lived close to the household’s rhythms, traveled with the family, helped with children, and remained part of Mina’s intimate circle for decades.

`image src=https://npgallery.nps.gov/EDIS/GetAsset/16334da34bc34ddab8dbd9ab45ce01c5/proxymdres.jpg .left alt="Thomas Edison, Mina Edison, and Lucy Bogue visiting Harvey Firestone, Idabelle Smith Firestone, and others at the Firestones’ estate." caption="Thomas Edison, Mina Edison, and Lucy Bogue visiting Harvey Firestone, Idabelle Smith Firestone, and others at the Firestones’ estate, 1931. [Source: NPGallery, “Thomas Edison, Mina Edison, and Lucy Bogue visiting Harvey Firestone, his wife Idabelle Smith Firestone, and others at the Firestones’ estate.”](https://npgallery.nps.gov/EDIS/AssetDetail/16334da3-4bc3-4dda-b8db-d9ab45ce01c5)

Bogue’s presence helps show that household labor and household intimacy did not always fit neatly into job categories. Companions, teachers, secretaries, and trusted family associates could perform forms of care, supervision, emotional labor, and memory work that overlapped with domestic service while carrying a different social status. In the Edison household, Bogue appears as someone both inside and slightly apart from the servant hierarchy.

Lucy Bogue’s room was one of the most clearly individualized third-floor spaces at Glenmont. Its furnishings suggest comfort, permanence, and a status different from the more standardized servant rooms nearby. The room helps visitors see hierarchy within the upper floor itself. All staff and household associates did not occupy the same position. Some workers lived in modest rooms designed for rest between duties; Bogue occupied a more personal space that reflected her unusual closeness to the family.

Her room also shows why “rest and respite” cannot be interpreted as a single experience. For a chambermaid, maid, or cook, the third floor could mean rest within reach of the bell. For Bogue, it could mean belonging, privacy, and long-term trust, but still within the working world of Glenmont.

### Companionship, Service, and Unequal Intimacy

Lucy Bogue’s position in the Edison household can be better understood when compared with Lena McCarthy Doyle Phillips. Both women were long-term presences in the family’s domestic life. Both were trusted. Both moved through intimate spaces and relationships. Yet they did not occupy the same social position.

Bogue was often addressed with the respectability of “Miss Bogue” and appears to have had a more companionate relationship with the family. Her room on the third floor was individually identified and more fully furnished, suggesting a status closer to a resident companion, teacher, or trusted family associate than to an ordinary servant. Even affectionate nicknames such as “Boguelette” suggest familiarity without erasing her social respectability.

Lena, by contrast, remained marked by the language of service even after decades of loyalty. In a 1927 letter to Theodore and Ann Edison, Mina described Lena as a “faithful, loyal girl” who “really cares for us.” The affection is clear, but so is the hierarchy. Mina recognized Lena’s loneliness, aging, and devotion, yet still referred to her as a “girl,” a term commonly used for women servants regardless of age.

“It just seems impossible to get letters written. I started this and then Lena came upstairs, as I am having her help with the dinner tonight, and talked and talked and talked. But poor girl is pretty much alone and is getting old. She seems to be having a swelling on her neck, and a pain in her side. She has been a faithful, loyal girl and I think really cares for us.”

The contrast between Bogue and Lena reveals hierarchy within intimacy. Long service could create trust, affection, and emotional dependence, but it did not necessarily create equality. Bogue’s education, class position, and role as companion or teacher allowed her a different kind of closeness to the Edisons. Lena’s closeness came through care, service, and loyalty. Both women mattered deeply to the household, but the household recognized them differently.

### The Servants’ Dining Room
{: .wrap}

`image src=images/servants-dining-room-drawing-glenmont.png .right alt="Drawing of Glenmont’s Servants’ Dining Room, Room 105, showing a table and chairs, rocking chairs, fireplace, cabinet, side furniture, rug, and framed wall decorations." caption="Room 105, Servants’ Dining Room. Drawing of Glenmont’s first-floor servants’ dining room. From The House at Glenmont, Volume II: Furnishing Plan. Image credit: National Park Service, Thomas Edison National Historical Park."`

The dining room for domestic workers had many names over the six decades that Mina Edison managed the home. It was called the “Servants’ Hall” by *American Architect and Building News* in 1881. In an 1886 inventory, it was described as the “Servants’ Dining Room,” and painting estimates from 1905–1907 continued to use that name. From the early 1900s through 1931, appraisals shifted to “Help’s Dining Room,” “Helps’ Dining Room,” and finally “Service Dining Room.” These changing names suggest how language and social perceptions of service labor evolved over time, especially during transitions in household management and ownership.

Domestic staff used this space for both dining and leisure. The basic room layout remained largely unchanged from 1881. Furnishings included a table and chairs, two rocking chairs, a phonograph with records, a small bookcase, and, by 1930, a piano. Staff likely had limited structured leisure time, and this room was one of the few areas where relaxation was sanctioned. The inclusion of a phonograph, books, and piano suggests that the working environment of the house allowed some space for music, reading, and creative leisure.

In 1907, the architectural firm Allen & Collens proposed major alterations that would have removed the north and south walls and expanded adjacent rooms. These plans were not executed. Today, the room is furnished circa 1920.

### Were Edison’s Domestic Workers Paid Well?

Wage records from Glenmont suggest that Thomas and Mina Edison paid their domestic staff above the averages reported at the turn of the twentieth century. In *Domestic Service* (1901), reformer Lucy Maynard Salmon included wage data showing that cooks in the 1890s typically earned between $3.50 and $4.00 per week—about $15 to $19 per month. By comparison, Glenmont payroll records from 1912 show that Mary McMahon, the family’s cook, earned $45 per month. Around the same time, Henry Horsey, who worked on the estate grounds, earned $60 per month.

Even allowing for modest wage increases between the 1890s and 1912, these salaries were substantially higher than published national averages for domestic service. Domestic workers at Glenmont also received room and board, meaning their compensation included housing and meals in addition to cash wages. While domestic labor was often low-paid and unstable in this period, employment at Glenmont appears to have offered comparatively strong wages and long-term stability—reflecting the scale, visibility, and high expectations of an elite household connected to one of the nation’s most prominent inventors.

### Comfort, Order, and Expectations

Mina Edison had high expectations of the staff, but she also wanted the household to feel comfortable and well managed. She often left Glenmont for long periods while close relatives resided there, and her correspondence shows that she expected the house to remain orderly in her absence. In an 1889 letter, Mina wrote:

> “I hope that the girls or Mrs. [Conroy?] will leave everything in perfect order and that the house will look beautiful and clean.”

This expectation connected comfort to discipline. Clean rooms, orderly furnishings, and well-maintained staff spaces were not separate from the labor system of Glenmont; they were part of it. Rest and leisure existed, but within a household that expected readiness, neatness, and reliability.

# Conclusion

To move through Glenmont room by room is to encounter absence as much as presence. Parlors, bedrooms, kitchens, attics, service spaces, and grounds all bear the imprint of hands that polished, lifted, stirred, scrubbed, carried, planted, and waited. Yet those hands rarely appear at the center of the house’s official story.

Domestic labor was often designed to vanish. Beds were smoothed, fires laid, meals served, linens pressed, floors reset, gardens maintained, and guests welcomed so that order seemed to exist naturally. Looking closely at Glenmont’s spaces makes that illusion visible. It reveals how carefully coordinated, physically demanding, and emotionally complex this work was, and how deeply it shaped daily life on the estate.

Still, the archive remains uneven. Census records, vouchers, scattered letters, photographs, and newspaper clippings offer glimpses, but not fullness. We can sometimes know where people worked, what they were paid, and how long they stayed. We rarely know what they hoped for, what they endured, or how they understood their own labor.

To map their work across the house is not to complete their stories. It is to acknowledge that those stories existed—and that Glenmont’s history rests upon lives still only partially seen.
